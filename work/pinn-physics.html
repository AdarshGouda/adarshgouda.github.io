PINNs for engineering physics

A practical comparison of classical finite differences and physics-informed neural networks for diffusion and nonlinear Burgers' equations.

PyTorch · NumPyPDE solutionFDM ↔ PINN
01 Problem02 Approach03 System04 Decisions05 Result06 Lessons

Problem

Can a neural network approximate the solution of a governing differential equation in a way that is useful to an engineer, and how should its behavior be judged against a familiar numerical method? The project focused on understanding the method, not treating “AI” as a substitute for numerical analysis.

Approach

I solved one-dimensional diffusion and Burgers' equations along two paths. A finite-difference implementation supplied a transparent numerical reference. A fully connected neural network was then trained with a loss composed from the governing-equation residual plus initial and boundary conditions.

  • Define the domain, governing equation and boundary/initial conditions explicitly.
  • Build a classical numerical solution to establish a comparison baseline.
  • Use automatic differentiation to form PDE derivatives and residuals.
  • Compare the learned solution across the domain, not only at training points.

The workflow keeps both solution paths visible. That makes discretization assumptions, optimization behavior and solution error easier to discuss in engineering terms.

Engineering Decisions

Keep a numerical reference

A PINN result is not self-validating. The finite-difference solution provides a comprehensible baseline.

Encode the equation

Training penalizes the PDE residual, so physics constrains the hypothesis space rather than appearing only in the dataset.

Test nonlinear behavior

Burgers' equation exposes sharp gradients and optimization difficulty that a simpler diffusion problem can hide.

Inspect the field

Point accuracy alone can conceal physically implausible behavior between samples.

Result

The experiments reproduced the expected solution structure for both equations and made the tradeoff legible: PINNs offer a mesh-free, differentiable approximation, while classical methods remain efficient, transparent reference tools for these low-dimensional problems.

Lessons Learned

  • Physics-informed does not mean physics-guaranteed. Loss weighting and optimization still shape the answer.
  • A classical baseline is invaluable for debugging, calibration and communicating error.
  • The promise of learned surrogates grows with repeated-query and inverse problems, not necessarily with a single small forward solve.
  • Useful engineering ML starts by exposing assumptions and failure modes.
